\begin{abstract}
Autonomous Active SLAM requires mobile robots to maximize map exploration while maintaining localisation accuracy under physical sensor and actuator uncertainty. Existing deep reinforcement learning (DRL) navigation frameworks rely on power-intensive GPU clusters and static reward functions, making them impractical for resource-constrained mobile robots. We present \textbf{OmniRay}, an ultra-lightweight, CPU-native DRL framework for Active SLAM that combines a cross-platform C++ SIMD raycaster (supporting x86\_64 AVX2 and ARM64 NEON at $37\,\mu\text{s}$ per scan) with a 5-layer self-adaptive autonomy stack (health monitoring, dynamic reward adaptation, meta-policy tuning, curriculum management, and continual learning). OmniRay achieves 35--47 FPS policy training directly on a consumer ultrabook CPU ($<$15\,W) without GPU acceleration. Multi-seed benchmarks across seven models reveal that OmniRay learns an emergent spiral-like trajectory that eliminates discrete turn-in-place slipping, achieving $90.87 \pm 3.29\%$ mean coverage on the Intel Lab floorplan with 67.6\% fewer collisions than Yamauchi's frontier baseline. Zero-shot transfer to the unseen MIT Stata Center yields $91.08 \pm 3.99\%$ coverage without retraining, while maintaining constant-time $O(1)$ policy decision latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autonomous exploration and mapping in unknown environments (\textit{Active SLAM}) is foundational for mobile robotics in search-and-rescue, planetary exploration, and inspection~\cite{thrun2005}. Active SLAM requires simultaneously maximizing map coverage and minimizing localisation uncertainty. Classical methods like Yamauchi's frontier exploration~\cite{yamauchi1997} compute deterministic paths toward frontier centroids, but suffer from two key failure modes: (i)~\textit{kinodynamic instability}, where discrete stop-rotate-drive maneuvers amplify rotational errors under physical wheel slip, triggering wall collisions; and (ii)~\textit{brittle heuristics} unable to adapt to sensor dropouts or particle drift.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) offers reactive, end-to-end policy learning, but modern frameworks (e.g., Habitat~\cite{savva2019}, Isaac Gym~\cite{makoviychuk2021}) depend on high-power GPU clusters consuming hundreds of watts, precluding on-device edge training. Furthermore, standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2017} requires millions of interactions to balance obstacle avoidance and exploration simultaneously.

To overcome these constraints, we propose \textbf{OmniRay}, an ultra-lightweight, CPU-native Active SLAM framework.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{5-layer self-adaptive autonomy architecture} that dynamically modulates environment difficulty, reward weights, and policy parameters during training and deployment.
    \item A \textbf{C++ AVX2/NEON SIMD parallel raycaster} evaluating 128 LiDAR rays against arbitrary 2D wall segments in $37\,\mu\text{s}$ per scan---a $25.9\times$ speedup over scalar C++ execution.
    \item A \textbf{rigorous multi-seed benchmark}: an 18-run ablation matrix (900{,}000 steps), a 7-model benchmark on the Intel Lab, and zero-shot transfer on the MIT Stata Center.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Classical \& Information-Theoretic Exploration}
Frontier exploration~\cite{yamauchi1997} and vector field histograms~\cite{borenstein1991} guide robots to the boundaries of unknown space. Stachniss et~al.~\cite{stachniss2005} introduced information-theoretic planning that minimizes map entropy and particle covariance, while Umari and Mukhopadhyay~\cite{umari2017} developed RRT-Exploration for stochastic frontier detection. However, as noted in active SLAM surveys~\cite{cadena2016,placed2023}, when sensor noise causes state-estimator drift, classical heuristics frequently stall near obstacle boundaries.

\subsection{Learning-Based Active SLAM}
Chaplot et~al.~\cite{chaplot2020} introduced Active Neural SLAM using hierarchical networks, while Niroui et~al.~\cite{niroui2019} coupled PPO with frontier exploration in cluttered ruins. Nonetheless, existing DRL methods assume noise-free kinematics during early rollouts, rely on GPU infrastructure, and lack runtime health monitoring. Although Narvekar et~al.~\cite{narvekar2020} formalized curriculum learning, its application to CPU-constrained robotics remains largely unexplored.

\subsection{Gap Addressed by OmniRay}
Prior works treat reward shaping, curriculum schedules, and replay as static offline hyperparameters. OmniRay introduces runtime health diagnostics to drive closed-loop multi-layer adaptation, enabling stable, GPU-free Active SLAM directly on edge CPUs.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{POMDP Formulation}
We model Active SLAM as a POMDP $\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \Omega, \mathcal{O}, \gamma \rangle$~\cite{kaelbling1998}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State} $s_t = \langle \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{m}, \mathcal{E}_{\text{known}} \rangle$: true pose, static map, and explored cells.
    \item \textbf{Observation} $o_t = \langle M_{\text{cov}}^{(t)}, M_{\text{slam}}^{(t)}, z_t, p_t \rangle$: binary coverage grid $M_{\text{cov}} \in \{0,1\}^{100\times100}$, SLAM log-odds grid $M_{\text{slam}} \in \mathbb{R}^{100\times100}$, 128-ray LiDAR sweep $z_t$, and 5-D kinematic pose $p_t$.
    \item \textbf{Action} $a_t = [v_t, \omega_t]^T \in [-1, 1]^2$, scaled to $v \in [0, 1.2]$\,m/s and $\omega \in [-1.0, 1.0]$\,rad/s.
    \item \textbf{Reward}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:reward}
    R_t = w_e \Delta C_t + w_f \Delta F_t - w_c\, I_{\text{col}} - w_t + w_h H_t
    \end{equation}
    where $\Delta C_t$ is newly explored cells, $\Delta F_t$ is distance progress toward the nearest frontier, $I_{\text{col}} \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates collision, $H_t \in [0, 1]$ is the system health score, and $w_t = 0.01$ is a step time penalty. Base weights are $w_e = 1.0$, $w_f = 0.10$, $w_c = 0.10$ (scaled up to $3.0\times$ by L2 upon obstacle proximity), and $w_h = 0.10$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dual-Branch CNN-MLP Encoder}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{architecture_detailed_formulas.png}
\caption{OmniRay architecture: A 2D CNN processes coverage and SLAM grids ($2\!\times\!100\!\times\!100$) into $\mathbf{h}_{\text{2D}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{128}$; a 1D MLP encodes LiDAR and kinematics into $\mathbf{h}_{\text{1D}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{128}$. The fused vector $\mathbf{z}_t\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{256}$ feeds actor and critic heads trained via PPO.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The 2D CNN branch applies two convolutional stages ($2\!\to\!16$ and $16\!\to\!32$ filters, $3\!\times\!3$ kernel, stride 2) with batch normalization and ReLU, projected to $\mathbf{h}_{\text{2D}} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$. The 1D MLP branch encodes the 133-D LiDAR/pose vector via two linear layers ($133 \to 128 \to 128$). Fused embedding $\mathbf{z}_t = [\mathbf{h}_{\text{2D}} \| \mathbf{h}_{\text{1D}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ feeds actor and critic heads optimized via PPO's clipped objective~\cite{schulman2017}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clip}
\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}} = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \!\left[\min\!\left(r_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})\hat{A}_t,\, \text{clip}(r_t(\boldsymbol{\theta}), 1\!-\!\epsilon, 1\!+\!\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]
\end{equation}
with GAE parameter $\lambda = 0.95$~\cite{schulman2016gae}, discount $\gamma = 0.99$, entropy weight $c_{\text{ent}} = 0.01$, learning rate $\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$, and clip threshold $\epsilon = 0.20$.

\subsection{C++ AVX2 SIMD Raycasting Engine}
To eliminate perception rollout bottlenecks, OmniRay uses a custom C++ raycaster bound via \texttt{pybind11}. For $N_r = 128$ rays over $360^\circ$ against wall segments $(x_1, y_1) \to (x_2, y_2)$, 256-bit AVX2 registers (\texttt{\_\_m256}) evaluate eight ray-segment intersections in parallel:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ray_intersect}
t_{ij} = \frac{(x_1\!-\!x)(y_1\!-\!y_2) - (y_1\!-\!y)(x_1\!-\!x_2)}{(\cos\alpha_i)(y_1\!-\!y_2) - (\sin\alpha_i)(x_1\!-\!x_2)}
\end{equation}
with segment parameter $u_{ij}$ evaluated via vectorized cross products. Valid intersections satisfy $t_{ij} > 0$ and $u_{ij} \in [0, 1]$. On an Intel i7 CPU, this kernel completes in $\mathbf{37\,\mu}$\textbf{s} per scan vs.\ $962\,\mu$s for scalar C++ (\texttt{-O3 -march=native}), a $25.9\times$ hardware speedup.

\subsection{VectorSLAM Particle Filter}
Localisation uses \textbf{VectorSLAM}, a vectorized Rao-Blackwellized particle filter~\cite{grisetti2007} and log-odds grid mapper~\cite{elfes1989}. Motion propagation incorporates wheel slip ($\sigma_v = 0.10v, \sigma_\psi = 0.05\omega$). Scans update a log-odds occupancy map ($0.5$\,m/cell). Low-variance resampling executes when effective particles $N_{\text{eff}} < N_p/2$.

\subsection{5-Layer Self-Adaptive Autonomy}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{L1 Health Monitor}: Tracks $H_t = \lambda_e H_{\text{entropy}} + \lambda_c H_{\text{cov}} + \lambda_s H_{\text{slam}}$, flagging failure when $H_t < 0.50$.
    \item \textbf{L2 Adaptive Reward}: Multiplies collision penalty by $2\times$ and enhances frontier attraction when $H_t < 0.50$.
    \item \textbf{L3 Meta-Policy}: Contextual bandit MLP predicting continuous reward adjustments $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{reward}}$ from moving health diagnostics.
    \item \textbf{L4 Curriculum Manager}: Adjusts obstacles ($2 \to 12$) and noise ($1\times \to 2\times$) based on rolling performance:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:curriculum}
    D^{(e+1)} = \begin{cases}
    \min(1, D^{(e)}\!+\!0.05) & \text{if } \bar{R} > R_{\text{target}}, \\
    \max(0, D^{(e)}\!-\!0.05) & \text{if } H_t < 0.40, \\
    D^{(e)} & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{L5 Continual Learner}: Buffers episode trajectories and triggers 2{,}048-step replay fine-tuning upon sustained health drops ($H_t < 0.35$ over 5 episodes).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Noise Model}
Following sim-to-real benchmarks~\cite{zhao2020}, we inject: (i)~wheel slip $\sigma_v = 0.10 \cdot v_{\text{cmd}}$ and yaw drift $\sigma_\psi = 0.05 \cdot \omega_{\text{cmd}}$; and (ii)~LiDAR degradation via grazing incidence beam loss ($>75^\circ \to d_{\max} = 30.0$\,m) and 1\% stochastic packet dropouts per scan.

\subsection{Datasets \& Holdout Protocol}
Implemented in PyTorch and Stable-Baselines3~\cite{raffin2021}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training}: Procedurally generated arenas scaled from 2 to 12 obstacles under the Layer 4 curriculum schedule.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation (Zero-Shot Holdout)}: Unseen floorplans of the Intel Research Lab ($28\!\times\!28$\,m) and MIT Stata Center ($45\!\times\!35$\,m) under active sensor/actuator noise.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware}
All experiments run on an Intel Core i7 ultrabook CPU ($<$15\,W TDP) without GPU acceleration.

\subsection{Ablation Configurations}
Six configurations $\times$ 3 seeds (42, 43, 44) $\times$ 50{,}000 steps = 900{,}000 total steps: \texttt{full\_system}, and five single-layer ablations (\texttt{no\_health}, \texttt{no\_adaptive\_reward}, \texttt{no\_meta\_policy}, \texttt{no\_curriculum}, \texttt{no\_continual}).

\subsection{Baselines \& Implementation Details}
We benchmark OmniRay against six baselines under identical VectorSLAM noise profiles: (i)~Random Walk; (ii)~Yamauchi nearest frontier~\cite{yamauchi1997}; (iii)~RRT-Exploration~\cite{umari2017}; (iv)~Info-Gain $A^*$ (Stachniss et~al.~\cite{stachniss2005}); (v)~Shanghai AI Lab DRL~\cite{wang2024navformer} (a 2-layer MLP without curriculum/adaptive layers); and (vi)~DeepSeek-R1~\cite{deepseek2025} / Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{qwen2025vl} zero-shot VLM embodied frontier reasoning.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why Curriculum Learning Dominates}
Ablation results confirm that Layer 4 contributes $+16.2\%$ to peak reward. Exposing the agent to dense obstacles and maximum noise at step 0 inflates advantage estimator variance $\hat{A}_t$, destabilizing policy gradients. Progressive difficulty scaling ($2 \to 12$ obstacles, $1\times \to 2\times$ noise) stabilizes value convergence (explained variance $>0.50$), consistent with curriculum theory~\cite{narvekar2020}.

\subsection{Emergent Spiral Trajectories}
OmniRay discovers an emergent Archimedean spiral trajectory ($r(\varphi) \approx a + b\varphi$; Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectories}). Continuous forward sweeping ($v > 0$) eliminates sharp $90^\circ$ turn-in-place maneuvers that trigger severe differential-drive wheel slip, while keeping centripetal acceleration below tire traction limits. This allows OmniRay to reach $85\%$ coverage in $<110$ steps ($<3.7$\,s wall-clock) while classical frontier planners stall near obstacles.

\subsection{Coverage Efficiency vs.\ Perception Latency}
Info-Gain $A^*$ achieves higher path efficiency ($0.440$--$0.460\,\%$/m) via exhaustive shortest-path planning and costly entropy evaluation ($18.51$\,ms). In contrast, OmniRay prioritizes continuous forward velocity, accepting lower path efficiency ($0.200$--$0.217\,\%$/m) in exchange for sub-$4$\,ms decision latency ($0.038$\,ms raycasting kernel; $3.09$\,ms policy inference) and rapid early-stage coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:intel_benchmark} right).

\subsection{Lower Coverage Discussion \& Practical Merits}
Info-Gain $A^*$ achieves higher asymptotic coverage ($95.43\%$ vs.\ $90.87\%$) due to global graph search over distant corners, whereas OmniRay operates on a local sliding window and balances collision risks in narrow corridors within a 300-step horizon. Nevertheless, OmniRay offers decisive deployment advantages:
(i)~\textit{Deterministic $O(1)$ latency}: Inference cost is constant-time, avoiding the $O(M^2 \log M)$ scaling spikes of grid graph search.
(ii)~\textit{Graceful degradation}: Model-free reactive policies maintain forward motion under severe slip where graph planners stall in execution loops.
(iii)~\textit{Autonomous adaptation}: L1--L5 adapt to physical wear and drift without manual parameter tuning.

\subsection{Edge CPU Efficiency}
AVX2 SIMD raycasting ($37\,\mu\text{s}$) and vectorized NumPy SLAM ($<1.2$\,ms) sustain 35--47 FPS training throughput. A 50K-step model trains in ${\sim}25$ minutes at $<15$\,W, enabling direct edge deployment.

\section{Limitations \& Future Work}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{2D representation}: Extension to 3D octree representations (OctoMap/Voxblox) for aerial robots is planned.
    \item \textbf{Physical deployment}: Hardware validation on a physical TurtleBot3 rover in office environments is underway.
    \item \textbf{Architectural diversity}: Expanding benchmarks to broader multi-building architectural datasets.
    \item \textbf{Standalone baseline budget}: Evaluating unadapted PPO under identical budgets to further isolate layer gains.
    \item \textbf{Ablation comparability}: Acknowledging penalty regime variations in ablated reward telemetry.
    \item \textbf{Quantitative spiral fit}: Incorporating formal angular curvature regression to quantify emergent spirals.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We presented \textbf{OmniRay}, an ultra-lightweight, CPU-native Active SLAM framework for edge robotics. By coupling a 256-bit C++ AVX2/NEON raycaster ($37\,\mu\text{s}$ per scan; $25.9\times$ speedup) with a 5-layer self-adaptive autonomy stack, OmniRay eliminates GPU dependencies and sustains 35--47 FPS training on low-power CPUs ($<$15\,W). The learned policy exhibits emergent spiral exploration that avoids turn-in-place wheel slip, reaching $90.87 \pm 3.29\%$ coverage on the Intel Lab with 67.6\% fewer collisions than Yamauchi (1997) and zero-shot transfer ($91.08 \pm 3.99\%$) on the unseen MIT Stata Center at constant-time $3.09$\,ms decision latency. OmniRay provides a deterministic, low-power foundation for scalable edge Active SLAM.
